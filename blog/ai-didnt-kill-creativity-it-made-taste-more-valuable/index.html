AI Didn’t Kill Creativity. It Made Taste More Valuable.

When plausible options become cheap, the value moves to choosing the problem, setting constraints and rejecting competent but empty work.

By Ahsan Khan

In this article

  1. The bottleneck has moved
  2. Taste needs reasons
  3. Quality starts before generation
  4. More options create more responsibility
  5. Taste can be practiced
  6. Three criteria before the next output
  7. Evidence at a glance

The short version

  • Generative tools make plausible options cheap. They don’t settle purpose, fit, truth or consequence.
  • Taste is accountable judgment under abundance: choosing, explaining the criteria and owning the trade-offs.
  • Before you generate or choose the next thing, write down what would make it distinct, fitting and worth its trade-offs.

A blank page used to be the obvious creative problem. Now there is another one: a page filled with ten plausible answers.

Each answer may be clear, polished and usable. None is obviously wrong. Yet only one can become the headline, campaign, product direction or final paragraph. Having more options hasn’t made the decision for you. It has made the quality of that decision easier to see.

Generative AI helps people produce possibilities quickly. It can raise the quality of some work and give more people a workable start. It also changes where effort is most useful. When plausible output becomes abundant, value moves toward choosing the right problem, setting constraints, comparing alternatives, rejecting weak work and revising what survives.

That bundle of decisions is often called taste. The word can sound mysterious, as if a select group simply knows what is good. A more useful definition is available.

Taste is accountable judgment under abundance: choosing one option over another, explaining why and accepting the trade-offs.

The bottleneck has moved

Generation still matters, and AI has sped up parts of it. In a field experiment with 758 consultants, people using GPT-4 completed more tasks, worked faster and produced higher-rated results on tasks inside the model’s tested capability frontier. On a task outside that frontier, AI users were more likely to give a wrong answer.1 The technology improved production where it fit the work and created new risk where it didn’t.

Creative work shows a similar tension. In a preregistered short-story experiment, GPT-4 ideas improved ratings for creativity, enjoyment and writing quality, with larger gains among less creative writers. The assisted stories also became more similar to one another.2 Better individual results and lower diversity appeared together.

That doesn’t support a universal claim that AI makes everything converge. In a separate experiment with more than 800 participants, high passive exposure to AI-generated examples increased collective diversity, while individual originality didn’t improve.3 Different tasks, designs and measures produced different effects.

  • What the tools changed

    The cost and range of producing options.

  • What they didn’t

    Deciding which task deserves attention, and which output deserves to survive.

Taste needs reasons

Preference is personal: “I like this one.” Taste becomes useful when it can finish the sentence: “I choose this one because…”

Creativity research gives that sentence a foundation. A widely used definition treats creative work as both original and effective: novelty alone isn’t enough, and the work must also be useful, fitting or valuable in its context.4 That makes evaluation part of creative work. Divergent thinking produces possibilities; convergent thinking tests them against knowledge, purpose and reality. The two are complementary, and too much convergence can be as damaging as too little.5 A team that rejects every unfamiliar idea becomes predictable. A team that protects every novel idea struggles to finish anything coherent.

Accountable judgment holds both. It asks whether an idea is meaningfully different and whether that difference serves the work. It names the audience and the standard. It admits what will be lost by choosing one direction over another.

This is why “make it better” is weak creative direction. Better in what sense? More distinctive? Easier to understand? More accurate? Faster to produce? Safer to implement? A real criterion exposes the decision and gives collaborators something they can question and improve.

Quality starts before generation

Taste shows up in selection, but it begins earlier, with the choice of problem. A beautifully produced answer to a trivial question still spends attention badly. Before opening a tool or sketching a concept, ask what the work is supposed to change. Whose understanding, feeling or behavior matters? What evidence must the result respect? Which familiar answer is already overrepresented?

Constraints then turn that direction into a useful search space.

  • A writer

    Every claim supported by a source; every section answering one reader question.

  • A designer

    Accessibility first, a two-step task flow and the existing component system.

  • A marketer

    No fear-based claims, even if they would attract attention.

Constraints help on average, not always. Across 111 published and unpublished studies, constraints had a positive average relationship with creative performance, with substantial variation.6 Some limits focus effort. Arbitrary or excessive ones suppress it.

The practical difference is purpose. A useful constraint protects what the work must accomplish or forces a meaningful choice. A weak one exists through habit, hierarchy or convenience. “The reader must understand the recommendation in two minutes” defines a real design problem. “Use the format we always use” may only preserve convention.

Set direction before asking for volume. Otherwise faster generation just builds a bigger pile around an unresolved question.

More options create more responsibility

It is comforting to think one excellent option will announce itself. The evidence is less tidy.

People drift toward safe choices. When choosing among ideas they had generated, participants often favored feasible and desirable ideas over original ones. Telling them to select for originality changed their choices, while lowering satisfaction and perceived effectiveness.7 The study reveals a trade-off rather than one correct priority.

Judgment is real, and fallible. Students choosing their own most creative responses largely agreed with judges’ ratings, though the strength of agreement varied.8 Among circus professionals, creators predicted audience response to other people’s acts better than managers did, and lost that advantage when judging their own ideas.9 Experience provides useful standards. Attachment can distort how they are applied.

Rejection is productive here. It reduces noise and clarifies what the work is trying to become. Keeping every competent idea often produces a crowded result with no governing choice.

Taste can be practiced

Calling taste innate makes it hard to examine and easy to defend. The evidence doesn’t justify that fatalism. Across 70 studies, well-designed creativity training generally improved performance, especially when it taught cognitive skills and practical heuristics through realistic, domain-relevant exercises.10 That research covers creativity training broadly, not a validated faculty called taste. Still, it suggests component skills such as comparison, criteria use and evaluation improve with practice.

The practice is ordinary and demanding. Study strong work in context. Predict why something will succeed before you see the outcome, then compare. Keep a record of rejected options and revisit the reasons. Ask other people to apply the same criteria. Learn where your judgments are consistent, and where status, familiarity or ownership bends them.

Taste grows through repeated contact with consequences. It sharpens when choices meet readers, users, evidence and results.

Three criteria before the next output

Before generating or selecting your next piece of work, write down three criteria.

  • Distinct

    Is this meaningfully different from the obvious alternatives, or is it a competent restatement?

  • Fit

    Does it serve the actual reader, purpose, constraints and evidence?

  • Trade-off

    What does choosing it improve, and what does it sacrifice?

Define them first. Then generate options, compare them side by side, reject with reasons and revise the strongest survivor. Treat the three questions as a working practice, not a scientific scoring system.

Abundant generation doesn’t diminish creativity. It raises the cost of careless choice.

The work that deserves to exist will still need someone willing to say why.

Try it on real work

Write three criteria before the next output.

  1. Write down what would make the next output distinct, fitting and worth its trade-offs.
  2. Generate options, then compare them side by side against those criteria.
  3. Reject each losing option with a stated reason, revise the survivor and check it against a real reader or standard.

Taste, made accountable

Three criteria, written first

  • Distinct. Is this meaningfully different from the obvious alternatives, or is it a competent restatement?

Choosing among options

  • Put options side by side. Memory is a poor comparison surface.
  • Judge against criteria set first. Choose the criteria before attachment sets in.
  • Reject with a stated reason. “It feels off” is a signal to investigate, not a final explanation.
  • Revise the survivor. Selection identifies potential. Revision makes the decision coherent.
  • Check it against reality. An outside reader, user, editor or domain standard.
