The Difference Between Revising and Editing

Revision tests whether a draft says the right thing in the right order. Editing improves how the surviving draft says it.

By Ahsan Khan

The short version

  • Revision tests whether a draft says the right thing in the right order.
  • Editing improves how the surviving draft says it.
  • Run one structural pass before you polish sentences, so you don’t perfect a paragraph that is about to move or go.

You spend twenty minutes fixing a paragraph. You replace a vague verb, shorten two sentences, repair the punctuation and make the final line land cleanly.

Then you read the whole draft and realise the paragraph does not belong.

The problem is familiar because sentence work is inviting. It gives immediate feedback: a clumsy line becomes a clean one, and the draft looks better on the screen. Structural problems are less cooperative. They ask whether the section should exist, whether the argument holds and whether the piece is even answering the right question.

Both kinds of work matter. They are not, however, the same work.

The boundary isn’t absolute, and the terminology varies. But keeping the two questions separate can stop you polishing material that still needs to be moved, rebuilt or cut.

Revision changes the draft’s decisions

Revision reaches beneath the sentences. Rewriting sentences is one form of it, but the larger task is to reconsider the choices underneath. What is the piece trying to do for its reader? What is its central claim? Does the evidence support it? Does the order make sense? What has been repeated, buried or left out?

That wider definition has a history in composition research. In one comparative study, students tended to describe revision as rewording, while experienced writers worked across broader levels, including form, argument, examples and the likely effect on a reader.1 A related study of revision types separated surface changes that preserve meaning from changes to the text itself, at local or global levels.2

The lesson is not that experienced writers constantly tear drafts apart, or that every large change is an improvement. It is that stronger revision can reach beyond the wording to test the draft’s underlying decisions.

Imagine an article about documenting team decisions. Its outline begins with meeting fatigue, moves into a list of note-taking tools, and only later names the problem: months after a decision, nobody can recover why it was made.

No amount of line polishing repairs that sequence. Bringing the problem forward, deciding what a decision record must preserve, cutting what doesn’t serve that aim and adding an example of the cost: those are revision decisions. They change what the piece contains and how a reader meets it.

Editing improves the language that remains

Once the draft’s larger decisions are stable enough, editing turns to how paragraphs and sentences perform. Is the meaning clear in context? Are the words precise? Does each paragraph move without repetition or an unexplained jump? Does the rhythm suit the reader and medium? Are grammar, punctuation, usage and style consistent?

Editing isn’t trivial because it works at a smaller scale. A vague qualifier can overstate a claim. A buried subject can hide who is responsible. A punctuation error can change meaning. Sentence work deserves care, and it works best when you know which sentences have earned it.

The terms overlap, and that is normal

No single professional dictionary gives every editorial label the same boundary. One set of professional standards distinguishes structural editing, stylistic editing, copyediting and proofreading, and notes that stages can overlap, combine or carry other names.3 Another describes developmental or structural editing, copyediting and proofreading as the main stages of a traditional publishing workflow.4

The label matters less than the question being asked.

Even that distinction has porous edges. Changing one sentence can expose a contradiction in the argument. Moving a section creates transitions that need new sentences. Writers move among planning, producing text and reviewing as the work demands,5 so separating revision from editing is a way to direct attention, not a rule against moving between levels.

If a broken sentence stops you understanding the paragraph, fix it. If line editing reveals that the paragraph has no clear function, return to structure. The aim is to avoid investing heavily in polish while high-impact decisions are still unsettled.

Two passes: structure first, sentences second

Use two passes as a scaffold, not a law. The first asks whether the draft works as a piece. The second improves the language of the version that survives.

In pass one, make the large changes now. Replace a weak governing claim. Reorder sections. Add the missing example. Remove the clever paragraph that takes the piece somewhere else. New sentences only need to be clear enough to judge the rebuilt draft. This order follows a familiar teaching priority: focus, audience, purpose, organisation and development usually come before sentence structure, punctuation and word choice.6 “Higher” doesn’t mean nobler, and “lower” doesn’t mean optional. It describes the scale and likely reach of the change.

In pass two, read for the reader. Read difficult passages aloud. Check references after moving sections. Search for repeated words and claims, but don’t replace repetition mechanically: sometimes repeating a term is clearer than cycling through synonyms. Confirm that tightened claims still match their evidence.

Which pass does the draft need next?

Listen to the question you are asking.

If both levels are unstable, start with the problem whose solution would invalidate the most later work. That is usually a question of purpose, argument, evidence or order. If a local error blocks comprehension, repair it, then return to the larger decision.

Try it on one workflow

Before you change a sentence, run one structural pass.

  1. Write the draft’s central claim at the top of the document.
  2. Give every section a one-line purpose. Cut or rebuild a section with no distinct purpose, and combine two that do the same job.
  3. Only then edit the sentences that survived.

Two passes: revise, then edit

Pass one · Revise the structure

  • Purpose: What should the intended reader understand, believe or do after reading this?
  • Governing idea: Can you state the central claim plainly? Does each major section serve it?
  • Evidence: Which claims need support? Is the evidence relevant, sufficient and fairly represented?
  • Order: Does each section prepare the reader for the next? Has the conclusion appeared too late?
  • Omission and excess: What is missing? What should be cut, combined, moved or reframed?
  • Reader: Where would an unfamiliar or sceptical reader lose the thread?

Pass two · Edit the sentences

  • Is each sentence unambiguous in context?
  • Are the verbs, nouns and qualifiers precise?
  • Does each paragraph develop one useful movement of thought?
  • Can repetition, padding or unnecessary abstraction go?
  • Do rhythm and tone suit the reader and medium?
  • Are spelling, grammar, punctuation, usage and formatting consistent?
  • Has any edit changed the intended meaning?
