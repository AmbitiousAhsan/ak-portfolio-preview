The Hidden Cost of Making Everything Faster

Speed becomes costly when it removes the pauses where verification, judgment and original thought happen.

By Ahsan Khan

Faster Is Not the Same as Better

Speed is easy to measure. A draft took twenty minutes instead of an hour. A campaign produced twelve variations instead of three. A summary arrived before the meeting ended.

The harder question is what the clock leaves out.

Faster work is useful when it removes waiting, repetition or other low-risk effort. Haste is different. Haste compresses the moments when someone needs to check a source, compare interpretations, notice an exception or decide what is worth saying. The difference is not pace alone. It is whether faster execution leaves enough room for sound judgment.

Research supports that distinction. In an experiment involving bounded professional writing tasks, access to ChatGPT reduced average completion time by 40 percent and increased evaluator-rated quality by 18 percent.1 That is meaningful evidence for acceleration. It is also limited evidence. The tasks were short and specified, and the study did not measure the cost of factual verification after the draft was produced.

Pressure is conditional too. A meta-analysis of 76 experimental studies found that low evaluative stress sometimes improved creativity, while highly evaluative or uncontrollable stress tended to impair it.2 The analysis covered several kinds of stressors, not production speed alone. The responsible conclusion is not that deadlines destroy good work. It is that pressure can help in some conditions and harm in others.

Useful speed removes delay without removing thought. Harmful compression makes the time saving visible and the judgment cost easy to miss.

The Costs That Arrive Later

A fast output can look complete before its costs are visible. Those costs may appear as sameness, a weak decision or rework. None is inevitable, but each deserves attention when the work depends on interpretation rather than simple execution.

Consider originality. In a study of short fiction by nonprofessional writers using one model and format, access to generative AI ideas improved average ratings of novelty and usefulness, especially for less creative writers. At the same time, the resulting stories became more similar to one another.3 The finding does not show that AI makes all writing unoriginal. It shows a narrower trade-off: assistance can improve individual outputs while reducing diversity across a collection.

For marketers and editors, that distinction matters. A competent headline is not necessarily a distinctive position. Ten polished campaign angles may still share the same assumptions, structure and vocabulary if they began from similar machine-generated starting points.

Decision quality can also change under pressure. A systematic review of high-risk decision-making found that, in several professional settings, greater time pressure was associated with more automatic strategies, fewer cues considered and less proactive planning.4 Most of the reviewed work came from healthcare or other high-risk settings, and the findings were mixed. It would be wrong to treat that research as direct proof about an ordinary content calendar. It does, however, identify a plausible risk whenever a task requires people to compare evidence, anticipate consequences or detect an unusual case.

Rework is another possible delayed cost, but broad slogans overstate the evidence. One study in a software-development setting found that unresolved errors became substantially more expensive when they propagated and required redesign.5 A later analysis across 171 software projects found that delayed issues did not consistently require more effort to resolve.6 Software is not a proxy for all knowledge work, and even within software the cost of a late correction is context-dependent. An error that changes a single sentence is not the same as an error that invalidates a campaign premise.

The useful question is not, “Will every mistake become expensive?” It is, “If this judgment is wrong, what else will be built on top of it?”

A Test for Safe Acceleration

Before speeding up a step, examine five features of the work.

Stakes. What happens if the output is wrong? A formatting error and an unsupported public claim do not carry the same consequence.

Reversibility. Can the decision be changed cheaply, or will it shape approvals, published material or later work?

Ambiguity. Is there a clear correct result, or must someone interpret incomplete evidence and competing meanings?

Originality required. Is consistency the goal, or does the work need a position that is not merely a competent average of familiar patterns?

Visibility of error. Will a mistake be obvious at once, or only after other decisions depend on it?

Acceleration is safer when stakes are low, correction is easy, the task is well specified, originality is not central and errors become visible quickly. Deliberate friction becomes more valuable as those conditions reverse.

This is a decision test, not a scoring system. It does not produce a universal division between “human” and “automated” work. It reveals where the workflow needs stronger judgment and where routine effort can be removed without pretending that every step deserves equal caution.

What Automation Can Carry

Automation can improve performance without removing the need for independent verification. A systematic review of automation bias found that decision support often improved average performance while also creating overreliance and monitoring errors, especially under cognitive load.7 The review focused heavily on clinical decision support and predates generative AI, so it should not be treated as a direct measurement of modern writing tools. Its durable lesson is narrower: average improvement and new failure modes can exist at the same time.

A foundational analysis of industrial process control made a related point. When routine control is automated, people may be left responsible for rare failures while getting less practice and context for handling them.8 This is an analogy for knowledge work, not proof about writing. Still, it helps explain why “keep a human in the loop” is not a complete safeguard. Review is only useful when the reviewer has time, source access and a clear responsibility to challenge the output.

More recent evidence shows why task fit matters. In a field experiment at one consulting firm, people using GPT-4 worked faster and produced more, higher-rated work on tasks within the model’s capability frontier. On an apparently similar task outside that frontier, users were less likely to reach the correct answer.9 The study involved one firm, one model generation and a short-run task set. It does not define a permanent boundary for every tool. It does show that confident performance on suitable tasks should not be mistaken for reliability on all nearby tasks.

Hypothetical example: An editor uses software to clean formatting, standardize references and flag inconsistent terminology. Those jobs have clear outputs and are easy to inspect. The editor does not delegate the final source-to-claim check, because that step requires opening the underlying material and deciding whether the wording stays within what the evidence supports.

The point is not to preserve manual work for its own sake. It is to assign speed where correctness can still be seen.

The Friction Worth Protecting

Some pauses are empty delay. Others are part of the work.

A designed pause can improve performance on some creative and problem-solving tasks. A meta-analysis found that setting a problem aside before returning improved performance on average, especially for divergent-thinking tasks.10 Effects varied by task, preparation and the activity during the break. The evidence does not establish a mandatory cooling-off period, much less an ideal duration. It supports a more modest idea: immediate continuation is not always the best route to a better answer.

For knowledge work, four kinds of friction are often worth considering.

Verification friction forces a claim back to its source instead of accepting a fluent summary.

Interpretive friction makes room to compare plausible readings rather than settling for the first coherent one.

Editorial friction asks whether a correct statement is appropriate for this audience, context and level of consequence.

Originality friction creates distance from default suggestions before selecting an angle or position.

Hypothetical example: A marketer generates a first set of campaign angles quickly, but only after writing down several independent angles without assistance. The tool expands the field; it does not silently define the field. Before approval, the marketer checks the options for factual support, audience risk and convergence around the same familiar claim.

Not every project needs every pause. A routine internal update may need little more than a quick factual check. A public claim based on contested evidence may require source review, alternative interpretations and a second decision-maker. Deliberate friction should follow the judgment burden, not habit or nostalgia.

Protect One Slow Step

The goal is not a slower workflow. It is a workflow that knows what speed is allowed to remove.

Look at the path from initial material to final output. Find the step where a plausible mistake could survive, where several interpretations could fit, or where the first acceptable idea could displace a more original one. That is the step to examine before optimizing the rest.

Make its purpose explicit. A verification pause should require the underlying source, not a glance at a generated summary. An interpretation pause should compare alternatives, not merely reread the preferred answer. An originality pause should begin before the available suggestions become the boundaries of thought.

Then accelerate around it. Automate formatting. Reuse stable structures. Remove duplicate approvals. Generate low-risk variations. Speed is valuable when it creates more room for judgment instead of consuming it.

Before accelerating another workflow, identify one judgment-heavy step that must remain deliberately slow and protect it.
