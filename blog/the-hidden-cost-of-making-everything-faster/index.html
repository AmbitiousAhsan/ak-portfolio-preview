The Hidden Cost of Making Everything Faster

Speed becomes costly when it removes the pauses where verification, judgment and original thought happen.

By Ahsan Khan

In this article

  1. Speed is not the same as haste
  2. The costs arrive later
  3. Five questions before you speed anything up
  4. Hand tools the work you can check
  5. Four pauses worth keeping
  6. Protect one slow step
  7. Evidence at a glance

The short version

  • Speed helps when it removes waiting, repetition and low-risk effort.
  • It hurts when it squeezes out checking, interpreting and deciding, and those costs show up later.
  • Before you speed up a workflow, find the one step that needs judgment and keep it slow.

Speed is not the same as haste

Speed is easy to measure. A draft took twenty minutes instead of an hour. A campaign produced twelve variations instead of three. A summary arrived before the meeting ended.

The harder question is what the clock leaves out.

Faster work is useful when it removes waiting, repetition or other low-risk effort. Haste is different. It compresses the moments when someone needs to check a source, compare interpretations, notice an exception or decide what is worth saying.

»Useful speed removes

  • Waiting
  • Repetition
  • Low-risk effort

Haste compresses

  • Checking a source
  • Comparing interpretations
  • Noticing an exception
  • Deciding what is worth saying

The difference is not pace alone. It is whether faster execution leaves enough room for sound judgment.

Research supports that distinction. On bounded professional writing tasks, access to ChatGPT cut average completion time by 40% and raised evaluator-rated quality by 18%.1 That is meaningful evidence for acceleration, but the tasks were short and the study did not measure the cost of checking the facts afterwards.

Pressure is conditional too. Across 76 experimental studies, low evaluative stress sometimes improved creativity, while highly evaluative or uncontrollable stress tended to impair it.2 The responsible conclusion is not that deadlines destroy good work. Pressure can help in some conditions and harm in others.

Useful speed removes delay without removing thought.

Harmful compression makes the time saving visible and the judgment cost easy to miss. Every study in this piece has limits, and they are set out next to its findings in Evidence at a glance.

The costs arrive later

A fast output can look complete before its costs are visible. They tend to arrive as sameness, weaker decisions or rework. None is inevitable, but each deserves attention when the work depends on interpretation rather than simple execution.

Sameness

Assistance can improve each piece while making the set more alike. In a study of short fiction, ideas from generative AI raised average ratings of novelty and usefulness, especially for less creative writers. The stories also became more similar to one another.3 That does not show AI makes all writing unoriginal. It shows a narrower trade-off.

For marketers and editors, that distinction matters. A competent headline is not necessarily a distinctive position. Ten polished campaign angles may still share the same assumptions, structure and vocabulary if they began from similar machine-generated starting points.

Weaker decisions

Time pressure narrows how people decide. A systematic review of high-risk decision-making found that more time pressure went with more automatic strategies, fewer cues considered and less proactive planning.4

Most of that work comes from healthcare and other high-risk settings, so it is not proof about an ordinary content calendar. It does identify a plausible risk whenever a task requires people to compare evidence, anticipate consequences or detect an unusual case.

Rework

Late corrections can cost more, but not always. One software study found that unresolved errors became substantially more expensive when they propagated and required redesign.5 A later analysis of 171 software projects found that delayed issues did not consistently take more effort to resolve.6

The cost of a late correction depends on context. An error that changes a single sentence is not the same as an error that invalidates a campaign premise.

The useful question is not, “Will every mistake become expensive?” It is, “If this judgment is wrong, what else will be built on top of it?”

This is a decision test, not a scoring system. It does not divide work into “human” and “automated”. It shows where the workflow needs stronger judgment, and where routine effort can be removed without pretending that every step deserves equal caution.

Hand tools the work you can check

Average improvement and new failure modes can arrive together. A systematic review of automation bias found that decision support often improved average performance while also creating overreliance and monitoring errors, especially under cognitive load.7

Automation can leave people less ready for the rare failure. When routine control is automated, people may be left responsible for rare failures while getting less practice and context for handling them.8 It is an analogy, not proof about writing, but it helps explain why “keep a human in the loop” is not a complete safeguard. Review is only useful when the reviewer has time, source access and a clear responsibility to challenge the output.

Task fit matters. At one consulting firm, people using GPT-4 worked faster and produced more, higher-rated work on tasks within the model’s capability frontier. On an apparently similar task outside that frontier, they were less likely to reach the correct answer.9 Confident performance on suitable tasks should not be mistaken for reliability on all nearby tasks.

The point is not to preserve manual work for its own sake. It is to assign speed where correctness can still be seen.

Four pauses worth keeping

Some pauses are empty delay. Others are part of the work.

A designed pause can help. Setting a problem aside before returning improved performance on average, especially for divergent-thinking tasks.10 That does not establish a mandatory cooling-off period, much less an ideal duration. It supports a more modest idea: immediate continuation is not always the best route to a better answer.

For knowledge work, four kinds of friction are often worth considering.

  • Verification friction

    Forces a claim back to its source instead of accepting a fluent summary.

  • Interpretive friction

    Makes room to compare plausible readings rather than settling for the first coherent one.

  • Editorial friction

    Asks whether a correct statement is appropriate for this audience, context and level of consequence.

  • Originality friction

    Creates distance from default suggestions before selecting an angle or position.

Not every project needs every pause. A routine internal update may need little more than a quick factual check. A public claim based on contested evidence may require source review, alternative interpretations and a second decision-maker. Deliberate friction should follow the judgment burden, not habit or nostalgia.

Protect one slow step

The goal is not a slower workflow. It is a workflow that knows what speed is allowed to remove.

Look at the path from initial material to final output. Find the step where a plausible mistake could survive, where several interpretations could fit, or where the first acceptable idea could displace a more original one. That is the step to examine before optimizing the rest.

Make its purpose explicit. A verification pause should require the underlying source, not a glance at a generated summary. An interpretation pause should compare alternatives, not merely reread the preferred answer. An originality pause should begin before the available suggestions become the boundaries of thought.

Then accelerate around it. Automate formatting. Reuse stable structures. Remove duplicate approvals. Generate low-risk variations. Speed is valuable when it creates more room for judgment instead of consuming it.

Try it on one workflow

Before you speed up another workflow, protect one slow step.

  1. Look at the path from initial material to final output.
  2. Find the step where a plausible mistake could survive, several interpretations could fit, or the first acceptable idea could displace a more original one.
  3. Make that pause’s purpose explicit, then accelerate everything around it.

    Noy and Zhang, “Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence”

    Found: On bounded professional writing tasks, ChatGPT cut average completion time by 40% and raised evaluator-rated quality by 18%.

    Limits: Short, specified tasks. The cost of verifying facts after the draft was not measured.

    Byron, Khazanchi and Nazarian, “The relationship between stressors and creativity”

    Found: Across 76 experiments, low evaluative stress sometimes improved creativity; highly evaluative or uncontrollable stress tended to impair it.

    Limits: Covers several kinds of stressors, not production speed alone.

    Doshi and Hauser, “Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content”

    Found: AI-generated ideas raised rated novelty and usefulness of short stories, most for less creative writers, but made the stories more similar to one another.

    Limits: Nonprofessional writers, one model, one format.

    Reale et al., “Decision-Making During High-Risk Events”

    Found: Greater time pressure went with more automatic strategies, fewer cues considered and less proactive planning.

    Found: Unresolved errors became substantially more expensive when they propagated and required redesign.

    Found: Across 171 software projects, delayed issues did not consistently require more effort to resolve.

    Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt, “Automation bias”

    Found: Decision support often improved average performance while also creating overreliance and monitoring errors, especially under cognitive load.

    Found: People using GPT-4 worked faster and produced more, higher-rated work inside the model’s capability frontier, and were less likely to reach the correct answer on a similar task outside it.

    Limits: One firm, one model generation, a short-run task set. Not a permanent boundary for every tool.

    Sio and Ormerod, “Does incubation enhance problem solving?”
