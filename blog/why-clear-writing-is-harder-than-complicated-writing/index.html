Why Clear Writing Is Harder Than Complicated Writing

Clear writing is harder because it has to decide what matters, what comes first and what can safely go.

By Ahsan Khan

In this article

  5. One dense update, rebuilt

The short version

  • Dense prose can preserve every caveat and still hide an unresolved thought.
  • Clarity isn’t simpler vocabulary. It is guidance: the right route through the complexity for one reader.
  • To get there, rebuild a passage around the reader’s question, the idea that answers it and only the details that serve it.

A polished paragraph can still leave the reader lost

Consider this sentence:

In light of the multidimensional nature of the operational environment, the proposed implementation framework should be understood as an iterative mechanism through which relevant stakeholders may pursue contextually appropriate outcomes, subject to the ongoing evaluation of emergent constraints.

It sounds careful. It may even sound rigorous. Yet after reading it, a reasonable person could still ask: What is being proposed? Who needs to do what? Which constraints matter? What decision follows?

The problem isn’t length or long words. It’s that nothing leads. The writer hasn’t made the relationships visible. Context, process, participants, conditions and outcomes are all preserved at the same level.

Complicated writing makes room for this kind of avoidance. A writer can keep adding abstractions and caveats without choosing a governing idea. Clear writing removes that shelter. It asks the writer to decide what matters, what comes first and what the reader can safely ignore for now.

Clarity is the result of decisions made before and between sentences, not a cosmetic layer applied afterward.

Dense prose does not prove deep thought

Density has legitimate uses. A specialist writing for other specialists may need technical terms, compressed references and distinctions that would slow a general reader. A difficult idea may stay difficult after an excellent explanation. Readers with little prior knowledge tend to need explicit connections, while knowledgeable readers may sometimes benefit from making inferences themselves.1

So dense writing isn’t automatically confused writing. But density can hide unresolved thought, and three habits often expose the difference.

  • Abstraction without an anchor

    Words like strategy, alignment or optimization may be useful, but they don’t show what changed, who acted or what follows.

  • Accumulation

    Every fact goes in because choosing feels risky. Yet relevance depends on the reader’s question.

  • Defensive qualification

    Every possible objection arrives before the main claim. The reader meets protection before meaning.

Accumulation has a measurable cost. Interesting but irrelevant details reduced learning on average, though effects varied widely by context.2 Each detail has to earn its place in this explanation.

These habits often come from care. Experts know where the complications live, but may misjudge what a newcomer knows: learning something can make estimates of other people’s knowledge less well calibrated.3 Clear writing needs deliberate decisions about the audience and, when possible, testing.

Clarity is guidance, not simplification

Clarity doesn’t mean removing complexity until every claim sounds certain. It means helping a particular reader move through the complexity accurately. The writer is deciding which route this reader needs through the subject.

That route may need a technical term, a long sentence that keeps conditions together, or an uncertainty range that could change the decision. U.S. plain language guidance begins with audience and purpose, and rejects the idea that plain language means writing for children.4 A paper for physicists and a public information leaflet demand different forms of clarity.

Clarity also allows honest uncertainty. Stating uncertainty increased how uncertain people judged a finding to be, as intended, but usually caused only a small decline in trust.5 How much depends on the format and the type of uncertainty.6 A numerical range is not the same as saying experts disagree, and neither should be buried just to make a sentence feel clean.

Oversimplification deletes information that could change the reader’s interpretation or action. Clarity keeps that information and gives it the right position.

Six decisions rebuild a dense passage

Rewriting for clarity starts before sentence polishing.

Order matters because rebuilding structure costs effort. When readers have to hold disconnected pieces and reconstruct how they relate, that effort is unavailable for understanding the subject.7

Signals help, within limits. Headings, previews and summaries can help readers notice and remember important relationships, though no signal can rescue an incoherent argument.8 A heading should reveal the shape of the thought.

Every choice creates an omission. The writer has to tell a safe omission from a distortion. Explaining a mechanism step by step can expose gaps that familiarity conceals.9 Compression should follow understanding.

One dense update, rebuilt

The reader’s question is: “Why is the release moving, and what happens next?”

Every fact in the first version may be true. It’s still hard to use: the decision arrives last, causes compete for attention and qualifications come before the claim they limit. The rebuild follows the same six decisions: question, governing idea, dependencies, relevant detail, necessary qualification and a visible next action.

Clarity respects the truth and the reader

A complicated draft can hand the work of selection to the reader. It says, in effect, “Here is everything; work out what matters.” A clear draft takes that work on, without pretending the subject is simpler than it is.

Remove too little and the reader has to excavate the point. Remove too much and the point becomes false.

Good clear writing sits between those failures. It keeps the complexity that changes meaning and removes the friction that doesn’t.

Needless complexity can even undercut authority. In a series of experiments, unnecessarily complex vocabulary lowered judgments of authors’ intelligence rather than raising them.10 That doesn’t make simple words inherently superior or fluent claims more truthful. It does puncture the idea that difficulty is itself evidence of rigor.

The better test is whether the reader can see the reasoning, understand the limits and know what follows. That usually takes more thought from the writer, so the reader can spend their effort on the idea rather than on decoding it.

Try it on one workflow

Take one dense passage and rebuild it.

  2. Write the governing idea that answers it.

Rebuild a dense passage

Six decisions, in order

  • Name the reader’s question. Not the broad topic. “Why is the release date moving?” gives the explanation a job.
  • Choose the governing idea. The sentence that answers the question at the level the reader needs. Everything else explains, qualifies or supports it.
  • Order the dependencies. If B only makes sense after A, the reader meets A first.
  • Select details against the question. A fact can be true and still unnecessary here. Cutting it from the passage doesn’t erase it from the record.
  • Keep the material qualifications. Keep a caveat that changes the claim’s scope, confidence or consequences, and move it next to the claim it limits.
  • Signal the structure. Headings, previews and summaries should reveal the shape of the thought, not decorate the page.

Three habits to catch

  • Abstraction without an anchor. Words like strategy, alignment or optimization may be useful, but they don’t show what changed, who acted or what follows.
  • Accumulation. Every fact goes in because choosing feels risky. Yet relevance depends on the reader’s question.
  • Defensive qualification. Every possible objection arrives before the main claim. The reader meets protection before meaning.
