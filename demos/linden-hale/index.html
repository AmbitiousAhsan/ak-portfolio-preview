Planning ahead

Plan now, so the people you love don’t have to guess.

A plan is a set of clear instructions for the people who come after you: who receives what, who takes charge, and who you trust to decide if you cannot.

Wills & estate plans

Documents that say who should receive what, who should take charge, and who should care for the people who depend on you.

Often involves
  • Drafting a new will or reviewing an existing one
  • Naming guardians for minor children, where local law allows
  • Checking beneficiary designations alongside the will

Start here if you have no plan, or your plan predates a marriage, birth, move or loss.

Working together

What working together looks like.

A clear sequence, so you always know where you are and what comes next.

  1. A first conversation about your family, your concerns and what you hope to achieve. You hear whether and how the practice could help — and how fees would work — before any work begins.

  2. A written summary of the recommended steps, the documents involved and what you will need to provide. Timing depends on your circumstances and local procedures, so it is discussed openly rather than promised.

  3. Documents are drafted and explained line by line, or estate tasks are worked through in order, with an update at each meaningful milestone.

  4. Plans are signed with the formalities your jurisdiction requires; estates are brought to an orderly close. Plans are worth revisiting after major life changes.

Plain-language glossary

Probate
In many places, the legal process that confirms a will and gives someone authority to settle the estate.
Executor
The person named in a will to carry out its instructions and settle the estate.
Trustee
The person or organisation that holds and looks after property in a trust for others.
Beneficiary
A person or organisation who receives something under a will, trust, policy or account.
Power of attorney
A document that lets someone you choose act for you, for example with money.
Guardian
A person named to care for children if their parents cannot, where local law allows.

General definitions. Terms vary by jurisdiction.

Questions

Frequently asked questions.

General answers, in plain language. Search them, or open the ones you need.

Do I need a will if I don’t own very much?

A will is about more than money. It can name the people you trust to handle your affairs and, in many places, who would care for young children. Whether you need one depends on your circumstances and the law where you live.

What is the difference between a will and a trust?

In general terms, a will takes effect after death, while a trust is an arrangement in which someone holds property for others and can operate during your lifetime as well. Which suits you depends on your goals and your jurisdiction; a first conversation is the place to explore it.

How long does probate take?

It varies widely with the size and complexity of the estate, the place where it is administered and whether anything is disputed. A responsible answer starts with the facts of the estate, not a number on a website.

What should I bring to a first conversation?

Whatever is easy to find: any existing will or trust, a rough list of assets, the names of the people involved and your questions. You do not need everything in order to begin.

Is anything on this website legal advice?

No. Linden & Hale is a fictional practice created for a portfolio demonstration. The information here is general, may not reflect the law where you live, and reading it does not create an attorney–client relationship.

Can I use the request form for something urgent?

No. The form on this demonstration sends nothing to anyone. If your matter is urgent or time-sensitive, contact a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction directly.
