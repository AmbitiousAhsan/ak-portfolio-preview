Planning ahead

Planning ahead for the people you love

What usually comes first

Most people begin with a conversation about who they want to provide for and whom they would trust to act for them. Documents come after those choices feel clear.

Helpful to gather

  • A rough list of what you own and owe — home, accounts, pensions, insurance
  • Any existing will or trust, even an old one
  • Names of people you might ask to serve as executor, trustee or guardian

After a loss

After someone has died

What usually comes first

Look after yourself and any immediate arrangements first. The legal steps usually begin with finding the will, if there is one, and understanding what the person owned and who needs to be told.

Some steps have deadlines, and they vary by jurisdiction. If timing worries you, speak with a qualified lawyer where you live promptly — not through a website form.

Helpful to gather

  • Copies of the death certificate
  • The will or any planning documents you can find
  • Recent statements and post, kept together in one place

Related: Probate & estate administration

Named executor

You have been named executor or trustee

What usually comes first

Read the document that names you, and pause before distributing anything. Understanding your responsibilities first protects you and the people you are acting for.

Helpful to gather

  • The will or trust document
  • Contact details for the beneficiaries
  • A simple log of decisions, dates and money in or out

Related: Probate & estate administration · Trusts

Illness or incapacity

Planning for illness or incapacity

What usually comes first

Think about who you would trust to make financial and healthcare decisions if you could not, and talk with them about what matters to you.

The names, forms and signing requirements for these documents differ from place to place.

Helpful to gather

  • One or two people you trust, and a back-up
  • Notes on your healthcare wishes and values
  • Any existing powers of attorney or directives

Services

Four ways the practice can help.

Each starts with understanding your family and what you want to protect. The right combination depends on your situation.

Estate planning

Wills and related documents that say who should receive what, who should take charge, and who should care for the people who depend on you.

Often involves

  • Drafting a new will or reviewing an existing one
  • Naming guardians for minor children, where local law allows
  • Checking beneficiary designations alongside the will

Start here if you have no plan, or your plan predates a marriage, birth, move or loss.

Probate & estate administration

Steady guidance for the person responsible for settling an estate — from confirming authority to act through to a final, well-documented distribution.

Often involves

  • Understanding who can act and what is required of them
  • Listing assets and debts and notifying institutions
  • Keeping clear records for beneficiaries

Start here if someone has died and you have been asked, or expect, to handle their affairs.

Trusts

Considered use of trusts where they genuinely suit a family’s goals, and practical support for the trustees who look after them.

Often involves

  • Explaining whether a trust fits your circumstances
  • Drafting and guidance on moving assets into a trust
  • Helping trustees understand and carry out their duties

Start here if you have been told a trust might help, or you have been made a trustee.

Powers of attorney & advance planning

Documents that let someone you trust act for you if illness or injury makes decisions difficult — prepared while there is time to think.

Often involves

  • Financial powers of attorney
  • Healthcare decision-making documents
  • Conversations about wishes with the people you appoint

Start here if you want your wishes known and your affairs protected before they are tested.

Process

What working together looks like.

A clear sequence, so you always know where you are and what comes next.

  1. 01

    Talk it through

    A first conversation about your family, your concerns and what you hope to achieve. You hear whether and how the practice could help — and how fees would work — before any work begins.

  2. 02

    Agree a clear plan

    A written summary of the recommended steps, the documents involved and what you will need to provide. Timing depends on your circumstances and local procedures, so it is discussed openly rather than promised.

  3. 03

    Prepare with care

    Documents are drafted and explained line by line, or estate tasks are worked through in order, with an update at each meaningful milestone.

  4. 04

    Sign, settle and revisit

    Plans are signed with the formalities your jurisdiction requires; estates are brought to an orderly close. Plans are worth revisiting after major life changes.

Our approach

Careful with your paperwork.Patient with your questions.

Estate planning and probate touch the most personal parts of a life: family, home, illness and loss. Linden & Hale is shaped around the belief that you should understand your plan, not simply sign it — and that a hard season deserves a steady, unhurried guide.

  • Listen first

    Families are different. Advice should start from yours, not from a template.

  • Explain plainly

    If a clause or a step is not clear, it is not finished.

  • Work precisely

    Small errors in estate paperwork can cause large problems later, so details are checked twice.

  • Respect the family

    Grief and planning both involve relationships. Conversations are handled with tact.

Questions

Frequently asked questions.

Do I need a will if I don’t own very much?

A will is about more than money. It can name the people you trust to handle your affairs and, in many places, who would care for young children. Whether you need one depends on your circumstances and the law where you live.

What is the difference between a will and a trust?

In general terms, a will takes effect after death, while a trust is an arrangement in which someone holds property for others and can operate during your lifetime as well. Which suits you depends on your goals and your jurisdiction; a first conversation is the place to explore it.

How long does probate take?

It varies widely with the size and complexity of the estate, the place where it is administered and whether anything is disputed. A responsible answer starts with the facts of the estate, not a number on a website.

What should I bring to a first conversation?

Whatever is easy to find: any existing will or trust, a rough list of assets, the names of the people involved and your questions. You do not need everything in order to begin.
