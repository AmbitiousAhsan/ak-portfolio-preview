Arabic coffee

Lightly roasted coffee, often brewed with cardamom and poured a little at a time into small cups. Across the Gulf and beyond, it’s how a host says welcome.

The story

Arabic coffee is a family of related coffee-making and hospitality traditions across Arab societies, not one fixed recipe or a single national custom. UNESCO’s multinational heritage record recognises that variation.1, 2, 4

What the record agrees on

  • Hospitality is the common thread. Ingredients, roast, utensils and etiquette vary by country, region and setting.1, 2, 4
  • UNESCO inscribed “Arabic coffee, a symbol of generosity” in 2015 for the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Oman and Qatar, and extended it to Jordan in 2024.2, 6
  • It is part of everyday hospitality, weddings and tribal gatherings, including the majalis held for reconciliation.1
  • How it’s made and served is passed down in families, learned by watching and doing.2

The method

UNESCO’s 2015 account describes one shared sequence: selected beans are lightly roasted in a shallow pan, pounded in a copper mortar, brewed with water, moved to a smaller pot and poured into small cups.2 That describes the inscribed tradition, not a universal recipe.

Emirati and Saudi sources document cardamom in some preparations. The Saudi source also names ginger, saffron, cloves and mastic, and records regional differences in roast colour and flavour.3, 4

How it’s served

In UNESCO’s description, older people and guests are served first, the coffee is poured with the right hand, and a drinker shakes the cup to say they’ve finished.1 Cups are filled only a quarter of the way and refilled; at least one cup and no more than three is the common practice.2, 3

Country by country

  • United Arab Emirates: Abu Dhabi’s heritage account describes sorting, washing, drying, roasting, grinding and brewing, with separate pots for boiling with cardamom, filtering and serving.3
  • Saudi Arabia: the national reference records wide variation in roast colour and added flavourings.4
  • Qatar: the Ministry of Culture names the tools: al-mahmas (roasting pan), al-hawan (grinder), al-dallah (coffee pot), filters and fannajeen (small cups).5
  • Oman and Jordan: both are part of the UNESCO element. Jordan’s 2024 extension stresses gatherings, conversation and shared news more than a distinct recipe.1, 6

These Gulf and Jordanian examples don’t define Levantine, Yemeni, Iraqi or North African coffee, which have traditions of their own.

A note on the name

Abu Dhabi’s heritage authority writes it Gahwa.3 This entry uses qahwa as a practical romanisation of قهوة. Pronunciation varies across Arabic dialects, so there isn’t one correct way to say it.

Ways people make it

  • With cardamom, as in many Emirati and Saudi homes
  • With saffron, ginger, cloves or mastic, depending on the region
  • Golden in central Saudi Arabia, darker in the north, lighter in the south
