Urgent care, the ER or your regular doctor? Where to go

If a life could be at risk or someone could be permanently disabled, it's an emergency: call 911 or go to the emergency room. For anything less serious, your regular primary care team is usually the best first call, and urgent care fills the gap when you can't be seen soon enough. Below is how the three fit together, what each may cost, and one Texas detail worth knowing before you walk into a building with "ER" on the sign.

Written by Ahsan Khan for a fictional practice. In client work, your clinician reviews and approves every article before it is published.

This is general information, not medical advice. It can't assess your symptoms. If you think you or someone else is having a medical emergency, call 911 now.

The short version

  • Emergency room (or 911): for anything that could threaten a life, a limb or long-term health.
  • Urgent care: for a new problem that needs attention today but isn't an emergency.
  • Your primary care team: for ongoing health, prescriptions, follow-ups, and a first call when you're not sure.

You don't have to diagnose yourself to choose. You only need to decide which kind of door fits the moment.

When it's an emergency

MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's health site, puts it plainly: if a person or unborn baby could die or be permanently disabled, it is an emergency. Its list of reasons to call 911 includes:

  • Choking, or someone has stopped breathing
  • Severe chest pain or pressure, or severe shortness of breath
  • Suddenly not being able to speak, see, walk or move
  • Sudden weakness or drooping on one side of the body
  • A seizure that lasts more than a minute
  • A head injury with passing out, fainting or confusion

The same page lists reasons to go to an emergency department or call 911, such as heavy bleeding, a deep wound, a serious burn, a possible broken bone with loss of movement, a severe allergic reaction with trouble breathing, a high fever with a headache and stiff neck, and poisoning or an overdose.

HealthCare.gov describes an emergency medical condition as one so serious that a reasonable person would seek care right away to avoid severe harm. That "reasonable person" standard matters. You aren't expected to be certain. If it feels like an emergency, treat it like one.

If the crisis is emotional, or someone is thinking about suicide, you can call or text 988. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free, confidential and available 24/7, including by chat.

When urgent care fits

HealthCare.gov defines urgent care as care for something serious enough that a reasonable person would seek care right away, but not so severe it requires emergency room care.

MedlinePlus gives examples of problems an urgent care clinic can often handle: common mild illnesses like colds, flu, earaches, sore throats and low-grade fevers, and minor injuries like sprains, minor cuts and burns, or minor broken bones.

Two practical points:

  • Not every urgent care can treat every concern. Before you rely on a visit, confirm the location can see your concern and your age group.
  • Urgent care doesn't replace a regular doctor. Ask how your visit notes will reach your primary care team, and whether you need a follow-up. Our urgent care page walks through what that handoff can look like.

When your regular doctor is the better first call

If you're unsure what to do and nothing on the emergency list applies, MedlinePlus suggests you call or contact your provider first. If the office is closed, your call may be forwarded to an on-call nurse or provider. Your insurer may also offer a nurse advice line.

Primary care is also the right home for the things that don't fit a one-off visit: a long-term condition, a prescription that needs reviewing, preventive care, or a question about a test from last month. Having a regular team means someone already knows your history when something new comes up. See what a primary care relationship can include.

What each option may cost

Cost shouldn't stop anyone from getting emergency care. Still, it helps to know the difference for everything else. According to MedlinePlus, treatment in an emergency department can cost 2 to 3 times or more than the same care in your provider's office, and your plan may charge a higher copay for the ER.

For true emergencies, federal law adds some protection. The No Surprises Act, which went into effect on January 1, 2022, protects people with most types of health insurance from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency room visits. Under its rules, plans can't require prior authorization for emergency care, and out-of-network emergency providers can't bill you more than your in-network cost sharing. Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE and some other programs aren't covered by this law because they have their own billing protections.

For a non-emergency visit, ask the practice and your plan about the specific service before you go. Our costs and coverage guide has a short list of questions to ask.

A Texas detail: freestanding ERs

Around Austin, you may notice emergency rooms that aren't attached to a hospital. In Texas, these are licensed as freestanding emergency medical care facilities: structurally separate from a hospital, and providing emergency care. They can look like an urgent care clinic from the parking lot, but they are emergency rooms.

Texas law requires these facilities to post a notice at the main entrance, in each treatment room, where you pay, and on their website. The notice must say that the facility is a freestanding emergency medical care facility, that its rates are comparable to a hospital emergency room, that it may charge a facility fee, that it or its physicians may be out of network for your plan, and that physicians may bill separately.

So, in a real emergency, go to the nearest emergency room. For a non-emergency concern, read the sign on the door first. If it says "emergency," you're choosing ER-level care and ER-level billing.

Before you head out: a quick checklist

For a concern that isn't an emergency:

  • Run through the emergency signs above. If any apply, call 911.
  • Try your primary care office or nurse advice line first.
  • Check that the urgent care location can see your concern and age group.
  • Check that the location is in your plan's network.
  • Look for the posted notice: is this urgent care or a freestanding ER?
  • Bring your medication list, allergies and your regular clinician's details.
  • Before you leave, ask who will follow up and how.

After the visit

Wherever you end up, the visit isn't quite over when you walk out. Results, prescriptions and referrals each need an owner. Our after-visit checklist helps you leave with the right questions answered, so the next step doesn't get lost on the way home.
