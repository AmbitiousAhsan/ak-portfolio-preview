Costs & insurance

Cost, before the chair.

What a written plan should show you, the four places dental bills most often catch people out, and when you’ll hear about cost.

1

For the clinic. Your written treatment plan, explained line by line before the patient sees their own. “What your plan may pay” stays visibly separate from your estimate.

The written plan

Read the plan, line by line.

Choose a line to see what it means and the question to ask about it. There are no prices here, on purpose.

Written treatment planSample · no prices

Prepared by a real practice for one person, after an assessment. Nothing on this sample is a price, an estimate or a coverage decision.

Line 1

What’s being treated

A plan should name every recommended service, not just a total. If a word on it is unfamiliar, ask for it in plain language.

Ask“What is each part of this plan meant to address?”

Line 2

Now, or can it wait?

Many plans can be staged. The dentist can tell you which parts are time-sensitive for you, based on an individual assessment.

Ask“Which parts need doing soon, and which could wait?”

Line 3

Stages and visits

Knowing the number of visits helps you plan time off, comfort needs and payment timing.

Ask“How many visits is this, and how long is each one?”

Line 4

Practice estimate

This is the practice’s expected charge for the plan. It’s left blank here on purpose: this fictional practice lists no prices.

Ask“What is included in this estimate, and what isn’t?”

Line 5

What your plan may pay

Your dental plan decides benefits. Some plans offer a pre-treatment estimate. It is still not a guarantee of payment.

Ask“Can my plan give a pre-treatment estimate for this?”

Line 6

What could change

If something changes once care begins, ask that the dentist pauses to explain the change and its cost before carrying on.

Ask“What could change this estimate, and how will I be told first?”

Line 7

Your decision

A plan is a proposal, not a commitment. You can ask for time, a second opinion or a different option.

Ask“Can I take this home and decide later?”

Line 8

Who answers billing questions

Know who to contact after the visit, before you need them.

Ask“Who do I ask if the bill looks different from this?”

2

For the clinic. The four dental bill surprises behind a lot of “why is my bill different?” calls: deep-cleaning codes, the yearly maximum, frequency limits and the white-filling downgrade.

Where dental bills surprise people

Four surprises, four questions.

Each one is a question for your dental plan or the practice before you book. Plans differ, so ask about yours.

Cleaning, or deep cleaning?

A routine cleaning and a deep cleaning (scaling and root planing) are different services, and plans often pay for them differently.

Ask“Which cleaning is on my plan, and why that one?”

The yearly maximum

Many dental plans stop paying once a yearly limit is reached. A long plan can run past it partway through.

Ask“How much of my yearly maximum is left, and would staging across years change what I pay?”

How often it’s covered

Plans often limit how many cleanings, exams and X-rays they pay for in a year or two.

Ask“Are today’s X-rays and cleaning inside my plan’s limits?”

White filling, silver price

Some plans pay for a tooth-colored filling at the rate of a silver (amalgam) one, and you pay the difference.

Ask“How does my plan pay for tooth-colored fillings?”

3

For the clinic. This sets the rule billing has to keep: if something changes mid-treatment, the dentist pauses and re-estimates before carrying on.

When you’ll hear about cost

Before, during and after.

  1. Before the chair

    • A written plan that names each service
    • The practice’s estimate for each stage
    • Time to check with your dental plan

  2. In the chair

    • If something changes, the dentist pauses
    • You hear the change and its cost first

  3. After the visit

    • What was done, in writing
    • Who answers a billing question
