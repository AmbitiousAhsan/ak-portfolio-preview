How often do you need dental X-rays? Safety, timing and the apron

There's no fixed schedule for dental X-rays. The American Dental Association says there is no "one size fits all" interval between them. The right timing depends on your oral health, your age, your risk of dental disease and any symptoms you have. Your dental team should be able to tell you why they're recommending an image today, and you're always welcome to ask.

Written by Ahsan Khan for a fictional practice. In client work, your clinician reviews and approves every article before it is published.

This is general information, not dental or medical advice. It can't assess a symptom or tell you which images you need.

Why dentists take X-rays

Some problems can't be seen during a regular exam. Dentists use X-rays to find damage and disease that a visual check would miss. Examples include a new cavity between teeth or a tooth that hasn't come in properly. X-rays also help your dentist track your oral health over time. That's why images from past years can still be useful.

How often: it depends on you

According to the ADA, how often you need X-rays depends on four things:

  • Your current oral health.
  • Your age. Children and teens may need X-rays more often because their teeth and jaws are still developing.
  • Your risk of disease.
  • Any signs or symptoms you or your dentist have noticed.

The order matters too. Current ADA guidance stresses a thorough clinical exam before any images are taken. A 2024 ADA expert panel recommends taking X-rays only when they will provide necessary diagnostic information. So a good routine is to talk and examine first, then image if needed.

New patient, or coming back after a while?

If a previous dentist has X-rays of you, your new dentist may ask for copies instead of taking new ones. Ask both offices to help forward them. If it's been years and you're not sure where your records are, that's fine. Just mention it when you book. Our guide for coming back after a gap covers what else is worth saying up front.

Are dental X-rays safe?

Dental X-rays use very low doses of radiation, so the risk of harm is very small. Today's equipment is designed to limit exposure. The ADA notes that dental imaging is a minor part of the radiation people get from all sources. It also asks dentists to keep each exposure "as low as reasonably achievable." Low risk is still not zero risk, which is why ADA experts recommend using X-rays in moderation.

3D scans are different

Your dentist may suggest a cone-beam CT (CBCT) scan, which makes a 3D image of your teeth and jaw. These scans typically deliver more radiation than conventional dental X-rays, though usually less than other CT exams. The FDA suggests asking:

  • Is the scan necessary?
  • Is there an equally useful option with less radiation, or none at all?
  • For a child, does the practice use size-based settings?

The FDA also suggests keeping a record of your family's imaging history.

If you are or might be pregnant

The ADA's 2024 shielding recommendations apply to all patients, regardless of age or health status, including pregnancy. The ADA also notes that studies of pregnant patients have affirmed the safety of dental treatment. Tell your dental team if you are or might be pregnant so you can plan your care together.

What happened to the lead apron?

You may notice there's no heavy apron at your next visit. In 2024, the ADA said thyroid collars are no longer recommended for any dental imaging. It also said thyroid and abdominal shielding should be discontinued as routine practice. The reason: aprons and collars can block the main X-ray beam, which can mean more images and more exposure. Modern digital equipment and a beam limited to the area being checked protect you better.

The Texas rule changed in 2025

The ADA notes that some state rules may still require certain equipment. Texas used to be one of them. The Texas State Board of Dental Examiners had a rule requiring lead aprons for dental X-rays. In 2024, it held a public stakeholder meeting on whether to remove it, and most speakers supported removal. The rule was amended effective March 13, 2025, and the current version no longer includes the apron requirement. Practices in Texas may now handle this differently. Whether you're offered an apron or not, it's fine to ask why.

Questions to ask before your next X-ray

  • What are you looking for with this image?
  • Can you use recent X-rays from my previous dentist instead?
  • Is this a standard X-ray or a 3D scan, and why that one?
  • I am or might be pregnant. Does that change the plan?
  • Is the imaging part of today's visit cost? Does my plan limit how often it pays for this type?
  • When do you expect I'll need images again?

For more on the cost questions, see our costs and coverage guide. It explains how an estimate, an insurer's decision and a final bill differ.

When it's not an X-ray question

Some things shouldn't wait for a routine visit:

  • Contact your dentist if you have a throbbing toothache that won't go away or a bump like a pimple on your gums. Swelling in the upper or lower jaw is a very serious symptom.
  • If your dental office is closed after an accident or injury, the ADA suggests going to your local emergency room.
  • Call 911 if you have trouble breathing or swallowing, or you think it's a medical emergency.
