Perimenopause vs. menopause: signs and when to talk to someone

Perimenopause is the transition: the years when hormone levels shift and periods become less predictable. Menopause is a single point you reach after 12 months in a row without a period. You don't need to know which one you're in to ask for help. If the changes are affecting your sleep, your mood, your periods or your days, that's reason enough to talk with a clinician.

5-minute read7 sourcesUpdated October 2026

Written by Ahsan Khan for a fictional practice. In client work, your clinician reviews and approves every article before it is published.

This is general information, not medical advice. It can't tell you what is causing a symptom. If you think you are having a medical emergency, call 911.

The short version

  • Perimenopause is the time before menopause when your hormone levels change. It lasts about four years on average but can last up to 10. It usually starts in your 40s, but it can start earlier.
  • Menopause is reached when you haven't had a period for 12 months. In the U.S., the average age is 52. The most common range is 45 to 55.
  • Postmenopause is the stage that follows. Researchers who classify these stages count it from the final period (STRAW+10).

In other words, you usually only know you've reached menopause looking back. Most of the changes people notice happen during perimenopause.

What perimenopause can feel like

Changes to your period

In perimenopause, your periods may be shorter or longer, lighter or heavier, or skipped. Researchers who stage the transition watch for two patterns (STRAW+10):

  • Early transition: your cycle length keeps changing by 7 days or more from one cycle to the next.
  • Late transition: 60 days or more go by without a period.

Those are research definitions, not a self-test. Still, they show why your own period dates are some of the most useful information you can bring to a visit.

Changes beyond your period

The Office on Women's Health lists symptoms that can begin in perimenopause, including:

  • Hot flashes and night sweats
  • Sleep problems
  • Brain fog and memory changes
  • Mood changes, anxiety and depression
  • Joint and muscle pain
  • Vaginal dryness and urinary changes (called genitourinary syndrome of menopause)
  • Changes in sexual desire
  • Simply not feeling like yourself

MedlinePlus also lists headaches and a pounding or racing heart. Not everyone has all of these, and how much they affect daily life varies a lot. A clinician can help you work out which changes are part of the transition and which deserve a closer look.

Can a blood test tell me which stage I'm in?

Not always. The researchers who defined these stages rely mainly on period patterns. They say hormone tests are supportive and should not be treated as required for diagnosis. A clinician may still order tests such as FSH or estradiol. If you are still having periods, those tests may need to be repeated, because hormone levels move around during the transition.

Pregnancy during perimenopause

You can get pregnant during perimenopause, even if you skip periods. If you want to avoid pregnancy, ask your clinician how long to keep using contraception. If you're hoping to become pregnant, or you're not sure, that's a conversation worth having too. Either way, it's your call, and the visit should make room for it.

When to talk with a clinician

Book a conversation if:

  • Symptoms bother you or get in the way of sleep, work or relationships.
  • You're spotting between periods.
  • Your bleeding looks heavy. The CDC's signs include changing a pad or tampon after less than 2 hours, soaking through one or more every hour for several hours in a row, periods lasting more than 7 days, or clots the size of a quarter or larger.
  • Your periods stop before age 40. The Office on Women's Health suggests asking about testing.

Don't wait on these

  • Any bleeding after menopause. Vaginal bleeding after 12 months with no period is not normal. See a clinician right away, even if it's only a small amount.
  • Very heavy bleeding with feeling faint or very unwell. Treat this as urgent. Call 911 if you think it's an emergency.
  • Thoughts of harming yourself. Call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available 24/7.

Options that may come up

There isn't one plan that fits everyone. Here are some options the Office on Women's Health describes:

  • For moderate to severe hot flashes, FDA-approved treatments include hormone therapy and nonhormone medicines.
  • For vaginal and urinary symptoms, low-dose vaginal estrogen is one option.
  • For depression and anxiety, cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness-based stress reduction may help.

Whether hormone therapy is right for you depends on your history. Talk with your clinician about whether it is safe for you, and check with them before you take supplements or herbs. This article doesn't recommend any treatment. Its job is to help you ask better questions.

A short checklist for your visit

The Office on Women's Health suggests telling your clinician which symptoms bother you most. Bring:

  • Your symptoms and how much each one affects your day
  • The dates of your last few periods, or how long it's been since the last one
  • Your health history, including any history of cancer, blood clots or heart disease
  • Any hormone therapy you've used before
  • A current medication and supplement list
  • The one question you most want answered

You can print our menopause conversation guide and bring it with you.

How a conversation at Juniper works

A menopause conversation at Juniper starts with the changes that matter most to you. You don't need a diagnosis to book one. If you'd rather start with a broader check-in, or you have a different concern, a gynecology visit is a good place to begin. Coverage depends on your plan, so our costs and coverage guide lists the questions to ask before you book.
