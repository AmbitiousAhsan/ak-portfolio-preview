Does insurance cover ABA therapy in Texas? A guide for families

In Texas, whether ABA is covered depends mostly on what kind of plan your child has. State law requires many state-regulated health plans to cover ABA for a child diagnosed with autism before age 10. Texas Medicaid covers ABA for eligible members under 21. But most Texans with a health plan have a self-funded plan, which follows its own plan documents instead of the state rule. Here's how to tell which one applies to you, and what to ask next.

Written by Ahsan Khan for a fictional practice. In client work, your clinician reviews and approves every article before it is published.

This is general information, not legal, insurance or medical advice. It reflects Texas rules as of October 2026, and benefits vary by plan. Your plan, the Texas Department of Insurance or Texas Health and Human Services can confirm what applies to your child.

Step 1: Find out what kind of plan you have

This one question shapes almost everything else. The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) describes two main kinds of private plans:

  • Fully insured. You or your employer pay premiums to an insurance company or HMO, and it pays the claims. TDI regulates these plans, and Texas coverage laws apply.
  • Self-funded. The employer pays claims itself, often with an insurer handling the paperwork. TDI says these plans are exempt from state laws under the federal law known as ERISA. The U.S. Department of Labor regulates them instead.

TDI also notes that most people with a health plan in Texas have a self-funded plan, and that a plan from your employer is probably self-funded. Don't assume the state mandate covers your child until you've checked.

How to check: TDI says to look at your insurance card. "TDI" or "DOI" on the card usually means the plan is fully insured. Wording that says the insurer "administers" the plan, or the letters "ASO," suggests it's self-funded. If you're still not sure, ask your employer's benefits administrator.

A self-funded plan may still cover ABA. The difference is that its plan documents set the rules, not the Texas mandate.

Step 2: If your plan is state-regulated

Texas Insurance Code Chapter 1355 applies to group health plans from insurance companies, HMOs and some similar carriers. For these plans, Section 1355.015 says:

  • Screening: The plan must cover autism screening at 18 and 24 months.
  • The age-10 rule: Coverage applies from the date of diagnosis, but only if the diagnosis was made before your child's 10th birthday.
  • What's covered: The plan must cover "generally recognized services" prescribed by your child's primary care physician in the treatment plan that physician recommends. The law lists ABA, along with evaluation and assessment, speech therapy, occupational therapy and physical therapy.
  • Who provides it: Services must come from a licensed or certified practitioner, or someone working under one's supervision.
  • A cap after age 10: Once a child is 10 or older, the plan doesn't have to pay more than $36,000 a year for ABA.
  • Your share of costs: Deductibles, copays and coinsurance can apply, the same way they do for other benefits under the plan.

So "covered" doesn't mean "free," and it doesn't mean no paperwork. Our insurance and costs page explains these terms and includes a blank worksheet for the billing conversation.

Step 3: If your child has Texas Medicaid

Texas Medicaid began covering ABA evaluation and treatment on February 1, 2022. Under Texas rules, a Medicaid-enrolled child qualifies when they are under 21, have an autism diagnosis and ABA is medically necessary. The benefit is part of the Texas Health Steps Comprehensive Care Program.

A few requirements shape the first steps:

  • The diagnosis: It must come from a qualified professional, and it must be based on a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation.
  • Authorization: ABA services must be authorized and based on an order, referral or prescription for an ABA evaluation or treatment.

If you're not sure whether your child's diagnosis report meets the requirements, ask your child's pediatric clinician and your Medicaid health plan before you schedule an assessment.

What about CHIP?

CHIP is a separate program from Medicaid. The rules above describe the Medicaid benefit. If your child is covered through CHIP, ask the CHIP health plan directly what it covers. TDI directs Medicaid and CHIP complaints to Texas Health and Human Services, not to TDI.

Coverage isn't the same as a yes

Even when ABA is a covered benefit, a few things still have to line up:

  • The specific clinic and supervising clinician need to be in network.
  • Any prior authorization needs to be approved.
  • Your share of the cost needs to be clear.

A diagnosis or referral on its own doesn't confirm any of these. Our getting-started guide shows who handles each step.

If your plan denies a request, ask for the reason in writing. HealthCare.gov explains that you have the right to an internal appeal, and then an external review by an independent third party. In urgent cases, the plan has to speed up the internal appeal. Self-funded plans also have to offer an appeals process, which should be described in your plan documents.

Questions to ask your plan or benefits team

  • Is our plan fully insured or self-funded?
  • Is ABA a covered benefit for our child? Do any age or dollar limits apply?
  • Which documents do you need? (For example, a diagnosis report, a referral or prescription, or a treatment plan.)
  • Does the evaluation or treatment need prior authorization? Who requests it?
  • Is this clinic, and its supervising behavior analyst, in network?
  • What deductible, copay or coinsurance applies?
  • How long does an approval last, and what's needed to renew it?

Write down the date, the name of the person you spoke with and any reference number. Our benefits and approvals guide has more questions to bring.

Coverage is one question. Fit is another.

Coverage tells you what a plan may pay for. It doesn't tell you whether ABA is right for your child, and it doesn't mean you have to use it. That's a family decision you make with your child's clinicians. Take time to understand the goals and methods, and how your child's comfort and choices will be respected.
