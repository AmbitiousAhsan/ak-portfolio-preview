The Kitewell care concept

Individual goals.
A shared understanding.

Applied behavior analysis, or ABA, is an approach to teaching and supporting skills. What is appropriate for a particular child depends on an individualized clinical assessment and a discussion of options.

Ask how the provider includes your child’s preferences, explains recommendations and checks that the experience continues to fit.

What care can involve

Picture the care.
Understand the purpose.

Illustrative sequences, not a fixed appointment schedule. The actual activities, length and frequency depend on an individual clinical plan.

Get a clearer picture before discussing a plan.

An ABA assessment informs care planning. It is not the same as an autism diagnostic evaluation.

  1. Your perspective

    Discuss your child’s communication, interests, daily routines and existing support. Bring the questions that matter to your family.

  2. Observation and activities

    The clinician selects appropriate ways to learn about your child. Ask what will happen, what it helps them understand and how breaks are handled.

  3. An explanation of the findings

    Discuss what was learned, what is still uncertain and whether this service is appropriate. Ask for unfamiliar terms to be explained.

  4. A proposed plan, with room for questions

    Review the rationale for goals and methods, the suggested commitment, alternatives and how care would be reviewed. A recommendation is a conversation, not a formality.

Work on an agreed goal, with attention to the child.

A session is part of an agreed plan. A room photo cannot tell you which methods a provider uses.

  1. Check in

    Share anything that may affect the visit and clarify what is planned. The team should explain who will be with the child.

  2. Activities linked to a goal

    An agreed skill might be explored through play, routines or other clinician-selected activities. Ask how the activity relates to everyday life.

  3. Pause or adjust

    Discuss how the team responds to communication, discomfort or a wish to stop, and how activities can change.

  4. An update you can understand

    Ask what was worked on, what the team noticed and what will be discussed next. Confirm how caregiver input reaches the supervising clinician.

Look at the evidence and the family’s experience together.

A progress discussion should include meaningful goals, understandable information and your family’s feedback.

  1. Revisit the purpose

    Which agreed goals are being reviewed, and why do they matter to this child and family?

  2. Explain the observations

    Ask the clinician to explain the information collected, its limitations and what it does or does not show.

  3. Add the family’s perspective

    Discuss whether any changes are useful in daily life, whether the experience is comfortable and whether the commitment remains practical.

  4. Decide what should change

    Ask whether goals, methods, support levels or the service itself should be reconsidered. Agree when the next review will happen.

Educational context: AAP guidance on individualized supports and BACB ethical standards. These examples do not establish Kitewell’s real-world practices.

02 / Goals that mean something

Connect the goal
to a moment in real life.

These examples are conversation prompts, not recommendations or promised outcomes.

Communication

“How does my child tell us what they need?”

Discuss the child’s current ways of communicating and what support could make their preferences easier to understand.

Everyday participation

“Which part of our routine is difficult?”

Choose a specific situation to discuss, rather than assuming the same skill or schedule matters to every family.

Comfort & choice

“How do we know when a pause is needed?”

Ask how staff notice and respond to communication, discomfort and a wish to pause.

Comfort, choice & concerns

“What if my child
doesn’t want to?”

That deserves a clear answer before care begins.

A child may communicate through words, gestures, actions or a device. Ask how the team learns those signals and how they affect participation, pauses and changes to a session.

Proposed care standard for this concept
How would a wish to pause be handled?+

The proposed standard is to notice and respond to the child’s communication, consider comfort and reassess an activity when needed. A real provider should explain its specific process, including how it recognizes assent, how it responds when participation changes and how safety situations are handled.

Can I question or decline a proposed method?+

The plan and consent discussion should explain the purpose, methods, possible benefits and risks, and alternatives in language you understand. Ask how to decline a proposed procedure, request a change or discuss withdrawing consent, and what that would mean for care.

I have heard mixed experiences of ABA. How do I compare providers?+

Ask about the actual goals and methods, rather than relying on the label alone. What would a goal improve in your child’s life? How are preferences included? How are discomfort and concerns handled? Ask the clinician to explain other appropriate options as well.

What should happen if I raise a concern?+

Ask for the name and role of the person who reviews concerns, how to contact them and how you will hear back. If something is not working for your family, the response should include a discussion of what to change, not only a request to keep going.

03 / Stay part of the conversation

Your observations
belong in the plan.

You see the moments that an appointment cannot capture. Discuss what feels useful, what is manageable and what deserves another look.

  • Ask for a plain-language explanation of goals.
  • Discuss how participation and comfort are considered.
  • Confirm who handles care questions between reviews.
  • Raise concerns when something does not feel right for your family.
