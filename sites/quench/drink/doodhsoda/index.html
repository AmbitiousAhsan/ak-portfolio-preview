Doodh soda

Cold milk, sugar and a lemon-lime soda, poured over ice. A summer drink in the milk shops of Punjab, and especially popular during Ramadan.

The story

Doodh soda, دودھ سوڈا, is exactly what the name says: doodh is milk in Hindi and Urdu, and the other half is soda.2 Dawn describes it as a mix of milk, sugar and soda, and the older milk shops in Rawalpindi use 7Up mixed with milk and crushed ice.1

How it’s made

Dawn calls mixing milk and soda water with crushed ice “an art.”1 Atlas Obscura describes one method: boiling milk goes into a metal bucket that’s twirled in a container of ice water, and once it’s chilled it’s mixed with the soda, whether Sprite, Seven-Up, Thums Up or the bright green Pakola. Other vendors pour the mixture rapidly from glass to glass over ice to aerate and smooth it, and some add a drizzle of pink Rooh Afza syrup. At home, milk, soda and a little sugar or honey in a glass will do.2

When and where

Dawn’s writer calls it a drink for the summer, sold at milk shops, makeshift stalls and samosa shops in Rawalpindi, though in winter shops make it with hot milk.1 Atlas Obscura says it’s especially prevalent during Ramadan, when the milk’s creaminess and the soda’s sugar and fizz make a soothing drink after a day without food or water. Gulf News calls it a staple in many Pakistani households, especially during Ramadan and on special occasions.2, 3 It also turns up in India: Atlas Obscura sends readers to a Kolkata eating house known for its doodh cola.2

Why people like it

Shop owners told Dawn that customers drink it for digestion, and it’s popular at breakfast alongside hot kulchas from the tandoor.1 In Pakistan’s Punjab and northern India, Atlas Obscura reports, people consider it healthier than plain soda, and it works as a cooling counterpoint to spicy food, like a light lassi.2

On the history

None of these sources documents who first mixed it. Atlas Obscura notes that milk with soda water was once seen as a healthy, nutritious combination in many places, especially Victorian England, and that the tradition continues in some areas.2 Writer Sadaf Ahsan calls doodh soda a long-time kitchen-table staple for South Asians, particularly in Pakistan and North India, and says it has colonial origins.4

Ways people make it

  • With 7Up or Sprite, the older shops’ standard
  • With Pakola, the bright green Pakistani soda
  • With almond milk or Rooh Afza
  • With hot milk in winter
  • As doodh cola, a Kolkata favorite
