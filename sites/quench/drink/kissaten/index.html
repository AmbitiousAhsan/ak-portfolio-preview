Kissaten hand-drip coffee

Coffee dripped by hand, one cup at a time, in a Japanese coffeehouse where nobody is in a hurry. Hot water goes through a paper or cloth filter in slow, small circles.

The story

A kissaten (喫茶店) is a Japanese coffeehouse, and the word means “tea-drinking shop.” The “kissa” part first meant drinking tea, and the shops only later became known for coffee.1 Pour-over and siphon coffee are the preferred methods in them, rather than espresso.1

The first one

Japan’s first modern coffeehouse is usually said to be Kahiichakan (可否茶館), which opened in Ueno, Tokyo, in 1888. Its owner, Tei Eikei, had studied in America. He wanted a social salon for common people and students, unlike the upper-class Rokumeikan. It served coffee, black tea, bread and sweets, and kept newspapers, cards, go and shogi on hand.2 It closed within a few years, but the modern kissaten came into its own in the Taishō era (1912–1926).3 At their peak, in the Shōwa era (1926–1989), there were over 150,000 of them across Japan.1

How it’s made

The All Japan Coffee Association gives recipes for two classic filters, both for one 140 mL cup from 12 g of coffee and 160 mL of water.

With paper, the water is left to cool to about 95°C. A first 20 mL wets the grounds for about 20 seconds, then 80 mL goes in small circles, then 40 mL when the water in the dripper has dropped by a third, then a last 20 mL.6

Nel drip uses a flannel cloth filter. The water goes in five or six pours over two and a half to three minutes. After use the cloth is rinsed with water only and kept submerged in the fridge, so its oils don’t oxidize.5 Perfect Daily Grind calls nel drip the method owners used to show their craftsmanship, and reports that siphons first went on sale in Japan in 1925.4

At the counter

Café de L’Ambre in Ginza, open since 1948, uses a cloth filter for single cups, serves green beans aged for years and roasts very dark.7 Many kissaten sell a weekday “morning set,” where the price of a drink also buys a small plate of food. Nagoya is known for the most generous ones.1

In the 1960s the word “junkissa” came into use for “pure” coffee shops that didn’t serve alcohol.1

Ways people make it

  • Paper drip, the everyday way
  • Nel drip, with a cotton-flannel filter
  • Siphon, a glass-flask method some shops still use
