Root beer

A sweet, foamy soda flavored with roots, barks and herbs. It started as a Philadelphia pharmacist’s “root tea,” shown off at the 1876 Centennial Exposition.

The story

Root beer is a sweet, foamy soda flavored with roots, barks and herbs. Before it came in a bottle it was a home drink: the Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia describes root beers made from roots, barks, flowers, herbs and other plants, boiled, strained and reduced, with yeast, sweetener and water added.1

Charles Hires

Charles Elmer Hires was a Philadelphia pharmacist who developed a powder that made root beer easier to brew at home. A 25-cent packet yielded five gallons, and the Encyclopedia dates it to the early 1870s.1, 3 Mental Floss tells the start differently: Hires found the recipe in a root tea served on his 1875 honeymoon.3 He first called it root tea, until his adviser Russell Conwell argued that miners wouldn’t buy a drink with “tea” in the name.2

In 1876 he showed it at the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia, giving away samples of the powder, showing how to mix it and selling glasses for three cents.1, 2 He sold it as “the temperance drink.”1 The Woman’s Christian Temperance Union still campaigned against it, until tests in 1898 found it had about as much alcohol as half a loaf of bread.3

What happened to the sassafras

Root beer was originally made partly with sassafras root bark and sarsaparilla, which foamed on their own.4 Federal rules now treat food with added safrole or oil of sassafras as adulterated. Safrole is the main component of sassafras oil, about 80 percent of it.5 After sassafras was banned in the 1960s, makers turned to flavors such as sarsaparilla, ginger, juniper, wintergreen, licorice, anise, cinnamon, citrus oils, cloves, vanilla and artificial flavors, and some added yucca extract for the foam.4

  • With wintergreen, licorice, anise or vanilla
  • With sarsaparilla or ginger
