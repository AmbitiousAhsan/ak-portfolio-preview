Do I need a referral for physical therapy or an orthopedic doctor?

For physical therapy in Texas, often not at first: a qualified physical therapist can evaluate and treat you without a referral for up to 30 consecutive calendar days. For an orthopedic specialist, there's no blanket rule. Whether you need a referral usually depends on your health plan and the practice. The catch is that "allowed by law" and "paid for by your plan" are two different questions, so it's worth checking both before your first visit.

This is general information, not medical or legal advice, and rules and plan terms change. If you have a serious injury, such as a bone pushing through the skin or a neck or spine injury with loss of feeling or trouble moving, call 911.

Three different "referral" questions

When people ask whether they need a referral, they're usually asking one of three things:

  1. Does the law let this clinician see me directly? This is a state rule. It matters most for physical therapy.
  2. Will my health plan pay without a referral? This depends on your plan type and terms.
  3. Does this practice ask for one? Some practices request a referral or records so they can prepare for your visit.

A "yes" to the first doesn't guarantee a "yes" to the second or third. Keep them separate, and the answer gets much clearer.

Physical therapy in Texas: what "direct access" means

"Direct access" means you can go straight to a physical therapist without first seeing a physician. According to the American Physical Therapy Association, all 50 states and the District of Columbia allow some form of direct access, though some states still attach conditions to it.

Texas is one of those states with conditions, and its rule changed recently. Since September 1, 2025, a qualified physical therapist in Texas may treat you without a referral for up to 30 consecutive calendar days, replacing the earlier limit of 10 consecutive business days. That change came from House Bill 4099. If you read an older article that says 10 days, it's out of date.

Not every therapist qualifies

The 30-day window applies only when the therapist meets the state's requirements. According to the Texas Board of Physical Therapy Examiners, the therapist must hold a doctoral degree in physical therapy or have completed 30 hours of continuing competence activities in differential diagnosis, have been licensed for at least one year, and carry professional liability insurance. It's fine to ask a clinic whether its therapists treat without a referral.

What you'll sign, and what happens after 30 days

Before treatment without a referral begins, you'll be asked to sign a disclosure form. Under the statute, it confirms that you understand physical therapy isn't a substitute for a physician's diagnosis, isn't based on imaging, a physical therapist can't diagnose an illness or disease, and your insurance may not cover the services.

To keep going past the 30-day window, the therapist needs a referral from a referring practitioner. If you think you'll need a longer course of therapy, ask early how that handoff would work.

Physical therapy and your health plan

State law sets what a therapist may do. Your plan sets what it will pay for. HealthCare.gov notes that in many HMOs, you need a referral before you can get care from anyone except your primary care doctor, and if you don't get one first, the plan may not pay.

If you have Medicare, the American Physical Therapy Association says that Medicare beneficiaries can go directly to physical therapists without a referral or a visit to a physician. There's still a paperwork step: a physician needs to certify your therapy plan of care. Ask the therapy clinic how it handles that.

Our physical therapy page covers what a first assessment may involve once the paperwork is sorted.

Seeing an orthopedic specialist

For an orthopedic consultation, the referral question is mostly about your plan:

  • POS plans: HealthCare.gov says these plans require a referral from your primary care doctor to see a specialist.
  • HMO plans: in many HMOs, a referral is needed for care from anyone other than your primary care doctor.
  • PPO plans: you can generally use providers outside the network without a referral, for an additional cost.

Your plan's member services line or member documents will have the specific rule for you. Even if your plan doesn't need a referral, the practice may still ask for previous imaging reports or notes so the specialist has the full picture. See what to bring to an orthopedic consultation.

When not to wait for paperwork

A referral is an administrative step, not a safety check. MedlinePlus advises calling 911 for an injury to the neck or spine, particularly if there is loss of feeling or inability to move. It also lists a possible broken bone with loss of movement, especially if the bone is pushing through the skin, as a reason to go to the emergency department or call 911. Paperwork can follow later.

Before you book: a quick checklist

  • Is this an emergency? If so, call 911 first.
  • Ask the therapy clinic: can your therapists treat without a referral in Texas?
  • Ask your plan: do I need a referral for physical therapy or a specialist?
  • Ask your plan: is prior authorization or a visit limit involved?
  • Confirm the clinician and location are in your network.
  • Gather any imaging reports, notes or postoperative instructions you have.
  • If you might need more than 30 days of therapy, ask who would write the referral.

Help the next team pick up the thread

If you do need a referral, the hard part often isn't getting one. It's making sure it arrives, with the right records, at the right desk. Our referral handoff guide covers what to confirm, and our costs and coverage page has questions to ask before your first visit. Either way, start with what you want to get back to. The paperwork is there to serve that goal.
